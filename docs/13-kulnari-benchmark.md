# Kulnari Mystery Golf: the benchmark library, what it teaches, and a logic check (26 Nov)

**Status:** research and analysis by Claude, from the team's visit on Tue 24 Nov 2026 and public sources. Nothing here is a decision. Facts about the venue are marked **[confirmed]** (a source is listed in section 8) or **[inferred]**. Sizes measured off video are estimates.

The point of this doc: Kulnari is the team's benchmark for "what good looks like". It holds a library of every Kulnari hole we have footage of, what each one teaches, a fact-check of the team's observations, and a catalogue of gimmicks. The New York holes that come out of it are in `14-new-york-iteration-1.md`.

## 4. What Kulnari teaches: ten patterns across the holes

1. **The theme is in the dressing, not the lane.** Most lanes are plain carpet, turf or floorboards with basic edges. The feel comes from props, wall finishes (faux brick, ivy mats, riveted panels), lighting and a sign on a lectern. For one night: keep lanes simple and spend on backdrops, props and light.
2. **Found objects make rails and obstacles:** chests (K02), planter crates (K10), bamboo (K14), a toppled table (K07), offcut blocks (K13). Nearly free, but anything loose drifts, so screw it down.
3. **Change the surface to change the speed:** grey carpet, a rug runner, bare floorboards, turf. Variety for free. (Our counter-rule: keep the main surface consistent so players calibrate once, and use a different surface only where it means something.)
4. **One big gesture per hole:** the quarter-pipe, the terraces, the pit, the furnace, the six cups. The best holes have one idea you can read from the tee.
5. **Reveals and stages:** the ball vanishes (K01, K05), the route moves into another room or level (K01, K04), the light changes mid-hole (K14). In an open hall, put the reveal where the waiting group can watch.
6. **Blending:** a shared brick-and-ivy palette across neighbouring holes (K06, K09), a balustrade looking down onto the next room (K04), two tees side by side in a shared courtyard (K11).
7. **Pace:** per player, a hole took from about 20 s (K02, K12) to about 95 s (K06). Long lanes and pits cost the most. Congestion is Kulnari's top complaint even at about 40 players an hour.
8. **Build details worth copying:** a quarter-pipe from ply skin on ribs, step-down terraces from framed ply boxes, a raised ply deck with a timber kerb, turf inlaid in curves, deep sleeved cups with liners, funnel rails into tunnels.
9. **Failures not to copy:** a gap that swallowed a ball (K11), a curved lamination peeling at its free end (K03), raw particleboard round a cup (K03 photo), chipped tunnel mouths (K05), battens low enough to hop (K01), curtains the ball rolls under (K04), and **rooms so dark players used phone torches on almost every hole**.
10. **The story keeps people busy while they wait.** Our "find the 11:59" does the same job and costs almost nothing, so keep it.

## 5. Logic check: the team's observations

The team asked to be fact-checked rather than followed. Each point gets a verdict, the reason, and what it means for us.

### 5.1 "Their small, low, interconnected rooms make the vibe easier; our big auditorium is like GTA 6 against The Last of Us"
**Partly right.**
- **Right:** enclosure removes "visual contradictions". Each Kulnari room hides the others, so every hole only has to sell one scene. Theme parks do the same with berms and walls.
- **But the analogy overstates it.** The Last of Us Part II's designers call it "wide linear": a guided path with open pockets. A hub with two looping districts (our layout v3) is exactly that shape.
- **Kulnari's mood leans heavily on darkness, and we can't copy that.** Players used phone torches on nearly every hole. At a church party with families, trip edges and a 23:00 crowd, that is a safety problem.
- **The big room has advantages Kulnari doesn't:**
  - one centrepiece everyone sees (the countdown tower)
  - spectators and midnight energy
  - marshals can see every queue
  - height for 3–3.6 m props
- **A city suits a big room.** Kulnari has to switch between 1920s rooms, a jungle and a farmyard. We have one theme, New York at night, so the job is one city with two neighbourhoods, not ten separate rooms.
- **How to get some of the small-room effect for one night:**
  - light each hole in its own pool under a dim warm wash
  - put a painted backdrop flat behind each tee, so players face their own hole and not the next one
  - hang string-light canopies or overhead "billboards" to lower the ceiling over each district
  - give each district a portal (a subway entrance, a theatre marquee) and its own floor markings
  - play quiet local sound per district
  - route the walk so each big prop is revealed on a turn
- See `14-new-york-iteration-1.md` for how the new holes use these.

### 5.2 "They used plastic hardboards with grass glued on: one thick slab of hard plastic with a hole cut in it"
**Wrong on the material, partly right on the method.**
- The cup close-up (`reference/kulnari/K03-riveted-curve-photo-only-2.jpg`) shows a torn edge with several plies: that is **wood sheet** (plywood or particleboard), not plastic. Plastic cuts to a clean, even edge.
- Inside the cut is a white **PVC pipe sleeve**, and the bottom is the concrete floor.
- The turf cup has a proper plastic liner with a separate bottom disc, about 105–110 mm across (regulation is 108 mm).
- Most Kulnari lanes are the room's own carpet or floorboards, or a raised ply deck with a timber kerb (K09, K12).
- Plastic would cost us more and flex more. In Singapore a 10 mm HDPE sheet of 1 × 0.5 m is about S$156, against about S$90 for a whole 8 × 4 ft sheet of marine ply.
- Our plan of 18 mm ply decks about 75 mm high is the right one.

### 5.3 "Our hole doesn't have to be deep. It can stick out slightly, as long as a fast ball that goes in doesn't roll out"
**Partly right on depth, wrong on the raised rim, and the fast-ball worry points the wrong way.**
- **Depth (partly right):** the cup doesn't need the regulation 101.6 mm. It does need to hold the ball fully below the surface with some margin: about 1.5 ball diameters, so 60–68 mm, plus a soft bottom. A ball rebounds strongly off a hard shallow bottom and rides up the far wall.
- **A raised rim (wrong):** to climb a step, a rolling ball has to arrive faster than a threshold:

  | Rim height | Minimum speed to climb it |
  |---|---|
  | 2 mm | 0.18 m/s |
  | 5 mm | 0.32 m/s |
  | 10 mm | 0.56 m/s |
  | 15 mm | 0.92 m/s |

  The gentle, dying putts are the ones most likely to drop, and those are exactly the ones a rim stops. A 10 mm rim roughly halves the useful speed range. A ball that does climb it hops, and can skip over the far side.
- **Fast balls lip out regardless of depth.** A ball dead centre over a 108 mm cup drops only below about 1.3 m/s, and 20–40 mm off centre only below about 0.8–1.2 m/s. Typical mini golf putts already arrive near that limit. The fix for "fast ball pops out" is not a rim. It is a deep enough cup, a soft bottom, and a backboard or a gentle uphill run-out behind the cup.
- **Recommended cup for a 75 mm deck with no hole in the floor:**
  - an offcut of 110 mm uPVC drain pipe (about 104 mm inside), or a commercial 108 mm cup cut down
  - 60–68 mm deep
  - a ply disc bottom topped with 10 mm EVA foam or carpet
  - flush to 1–3 mm below the surface, never proud
  - turf folded into the sleeve
  - about 300 mm of flat round it
- **If a raised cup is wanted as a deliberate gimmick (a "volcano"):** it needs a smooth collar no steeper than about 10° and at least 40 mm long, with no vertical face.

### 5.4 "The concepts look impressive, but it's really just wood cut to shape; not necessarily super complex"
**Partly right about the material, wrong about where the effort goes.**
- **Right:** the structures are simple carpentry or not built at all. K02 is the room's own carpet, K07 is a corridor, K13 is offcuts on concrete, K10 is crates. None of the mechanisms we saw needs an engineer.
- **But the effect comes from layers that people who haven't built sets tend to underestimate:**
  - a story and creative direction (an outside creative-direction contract reportedly ran about 15 months; unverified)
  - scenic finishing: aged paint, faux riveted steel, stained "teak"
  - dressing with real antiques
  - lighting, fog and sound
  - play-testing and repairs
- The fit-out cost was "seven-figure" (including a heritage renovation).
- **For us:** carpentry is roughly 30–40% of the hours. Budget at least as many volunteer-hours for painting (two coats plus drying time in Singapore humidity), graphics, lighting and play-testing as for cutting. One bold painted silhouette per hole beats many small props.

### 5.5 "Take lots of inspiration from their gimmicks; changing direction makes a small place more interesting and take more time; 18 holes took 2 hours"
**Right on the gimmicks and on changing direction. The 2 hours is a warning for a party, not a target.**
- **Direction changes:** they add interest per square metre (K01's two chambers, K04's split level, K06's L-bend). Do them inside a short footprint, though, not by making lanes longer: K09's long lane cost about 100 s per group with very little decision-making.
- **Kulnari is slow on purpose.** It is a paid attraction where long dwell feels like value, it admits only about 40 players an hour, and it still congests badly.
- **Our party has to move about twice Kulnari's rate on about half the holes:** 77–90 players an hour (`07-operations-and-safety.md`, `09-city-concept.md`).
- With every group playing every hole, the slowest hole has to clear a group of 4 in about 2.5–3 minutes. Kulnari averaged 6.7.
- **So every gimmick has to add fun without adding strokes, walking or retrieval.** Gimmicks that pass that test:
  - self-returning misses (a slope that rolls the ball back to you)
  - one shot each on target games
  - short tubes you can see through
  - the 5-stroke cap
- **The memory of "curves to guide the ball on hole 2" doesn't match the footage.** Hole 2 has no curve. The only curved floor rail is in the hole 3 photos (K03), and hole 4 has the giant curved ramp (K04). Either it was hole 3 or 4, or the curve is in a part of hole 2 nobody filmed.

### 5.6 "The rally hill, slopes of different heights, and tubes that trick you"
**Right: all three are good ideas, and all three are buildable.**
- **Terraces of mismatched heights:** this is K12, and it is one of the best holes in the library for us: quick, readable, all skill, cheap (framed ply boxes stepping down 50–80 mm, lips chamfered).
- **Rally hill (not in the clips we have):**
  - Keep the hill 120–180 mm high, with slopes of 10–15° and rounded transitions at the foot and crest.
  - A flat top 350–450 mm across, with the cup in the middle.
  - A miss rolls back to the player, so it returns itself, which is good for pace.
  - Steeper than about 20° turns it into a power lottery.
  - With two players at once from opposite sides, alternate turns so two balls don't collide.
- **Trick tubes (not in the clips we have):**
  - Use 110 mm drain pipe (about 104 mm inside) or 75–90 mm pipe for anything with bends.
  - Sweep bends only, never tight 90° elbows. A fall of 2–5°. Mouths flush with the deck.
  - Straight runs of 1.5 m or less, so a putter or broom handle can push a ball through.
  - One clear section, because players love seeing the ball.
  - "You think it's coming out in one spot and it comes out in another" is cheap drama.
- **Plinko (not in the clips we have):**
  - Pegs about 71 mm apart centre to centre, standing at least 35 mm proud.
  - A board at 10–15°.
  - Exit slots at least 55 mm wide.
- **Curling target:** 1 shot each, or everyone waits for balls to stop.
- **Caveat:** chance-heavy gimmicks (plinko, trick tubes, scattered blocks) are great equalisers between kids and adults, but they are seasoning. Two or three on a 10-hole course is plenty; the rest should reward skill.

### 5.7 "The holes don't intersect, but they blend so well they look great together even with different themes"
**Right.** Kulnari blends with:
- shared palettes (brick and ivy across K06 and K09)
- sightlines into the next space (K04's balustrade)
- two tees side by side in one courtyard (K11)
- continuous lighting

We already have the tools:
- the same rail, white cap and street-sign totem on every hole
- skyline flats on both side walls
- street markings and zebra crossings joining the holes
- a light colour per hole
- **New:** back-to-back flats that serve two neighbouring holes, and neighbourhoods with their own palette (see doc 14)

### 5.8 What the team didn't mention, but Kulnari shows
1. **Darkness.** Their mood relies on it; ours can't. The production question about a dim warm wash over the golf zone matters even more now.
2. **Ball traps.** K11 swallowed a ball. Our rule (gaps closed or at least 0.25 m wide; tubes with lift-off lids) exists for exactly this.
3. **Loose props drift:** K01's plank, K13's battens, K14's poles, K07's table. Screw everything down.
4. **Wear points.** Tunnel mouths chip (K05), curved laminations peel (K03), cup edges fray (K03 photo). One night is easier on materials, but hundreds of players hit the same spots. Face tunnel mouths with PVC or metal angle, and cap every free end.
5. **Uneven floors make balls drift** (a review complaint). Shim decks level and check them with a 2 m straightedge and a ball.
6. **Trademark detail in our own design.** The current totem uses "a round subway-style bullet in the hole's colour with its number" (`10-new-york-concept.md`). The transit authority licenses its line bullets, and a coloured circle with a number is very close to them. The risk at a private church event is low, but photos go online. Changing the shape before anything is printed is cheap (a rounded square, or a "2027 line" diamond).
7. **Permits and fire rules.** Check with the venue whether the event needs a temporary change-of-use permit, use flame-retardant fabric for any drapes, and **skip fog** (smoke detectors).
