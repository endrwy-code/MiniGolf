# What a carpenter needs, and the pack we've made (draft v1, 18 Nov)

**Status:** Claude's answer to the team's question on 18 Nov: "What would an actual carpenter need to build our course?" It covers what a carpenter needs and why, and how to read the drawings. It explains the materials and tools, and lists what we still have to find out. The pack itself is `carpenter-pack/countdown-golf-carpenter-pack.pdf`: 16 A3 sheets, generated by `tools/make_carpenter_pack.py` from the route C design in `11-route-c-carpenter-build.md`. A team member must read and own the pack before it goes to anyone. It's design intent for quoting, not engineered drawings.

## 1. The short version

A carpenter needs answers to five questions before they can quote or build:

1. **What am I building?** Drawings that show every part from above and from the side, with sizes.
2. **What from?** Materials, sizes and quantities, and who buys them.
3. **How well?** The quality level and what each part has to survive: a ball hitting it hundreds of times, people stepping on it, being carried by two people.
4. **Where, when and under what rules?** The workshop, the venue, door sizes, dates, and things that must not happen (nothing screwed into the venue floor).
5. **Who decides?** One person who answers questions and approves changes and spending.

The pack answers all five, apart from four venue facts we haven't measured yet (section 6).

**Drawing it all out also corrected the budget:** the timber comes to more than route C assumed. See section 8.

## 3. How to read the drawings

- **Plan, elevation, section.** A plan looks down from above. An elevation looks straight at something, from the tee or from the side. A section is a view as if the part were sawn through, so you see the layers (floor, plate, rail, surface).
- **Scale.** 1:25 means 1 mm on the paper is 25 mm in real life. Each view says its scale. But **trust the written numbers, not a ruler on the paper.** That's what "don't scale off the drawing" means.
- **Millimetres.** 1200 means 1.2 m. 45 × 95 means a piece of timber 45 mm thick and 95 mm deep.
- **Dimension lines** are the thin blue lines with slashes at each end. A row of them end to end along the top of a hole is a *chain*: each number is the distance from the line before.
- **Lettered tags** (A, B, S, T) point to the key on the right of each hole sheet. **Numbered circles** on the detail sheets point to the notes under each detail.
- **Shading.** Green is carpet grass. Diagonal green hatching is a raised green (65 mm up). Brown hatching is a 1:5 bevel (a ramp). Dashed outlines are props outside the rails. Grey bands are the rails.
- **D1–D9** are standard details. When a hole sheet says "rails D1/D2", it means build them as drawn on sheet 3.

## 4. Materials, explained

| Material | What it is | Where we use it | Good to know |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Plywood** | Thin wood layers glued with the grain crossing, so it's strong, flat and stable. Sheets are 2440 × 1220 mm ("8 by 4") | 18 mm: walls and boxes that take knocks. 12 mm: green tops, the cart front, the torch. 9 mm: signs, billboards, the proscenium. 6 mm: skins on cut-outs and flats, hidden plates | "Commercial" grade is fine for one night indoors. Marine ply is for wet places and costs far more |
| **Hardboard** | Thin pressed wood fibre, 3–6 mm, smooth on one side | Painted faces if it's cheaper than 6 mm ply | Doesn't hold screws, so it's no use for the hidden plates |
| **MDF** | Smooth board made of fine fibres | Avoid it | Heavy to carry, weak at screws, swells if it gets wet |
| **Solid timber** | Sawn wood, sold by old names: a "2 × 4" is about 45 × 95 mm once planed. "Dressed" means planed smooth on all faces | The rails (45 × 95), and frames and posts (45 × 45 "battens") | For rails, straight matters more than species. A bowed rail won't sit flat or rebound true |
| **Screws, bolts, glue** | Screws hold most things. Bolts or connectors join parts that must come apart. Glue plus small nails ("pins") hold thin skins to frames | Everywhere | "Countersunk" means the screw head sits below the surface, so it can be filled and painted |
| **Rubber strip and felt pads** | Thin non-slip strip; stick-on felt | Under rails, greens, cup liners and props | Stops sliding and protects the venue floor |
| **PVC drain pipe, 110 mm** | Standard plumbing pipe | Cup liners, cut to 57 mm | Cheap, the right size, and the ball can't climb out |
| **Sandbags** | 15 kg bags of sand | Ballast in prop bases, totems and signs | About 40, so about 600 kg. Fill them at the venue if you can |
| **Primer and paint** | Primer seals raw wood so paint doesn't soak in; then two coats of acrylic | Everything that shows | Volunteers paint. The carpenter only has to leave it filled and sanded |

**Why plywood rather than foam:** foam is light and easy to carve, but putters dent it, it needs a hard coating to last even one night, and a carpenter can't screw it down properly. Plywood is what a carpenter works fastest in.

## 5. Tools: what the carpenter brings, what we provide

**The carpenter usually brings:** a circular or track saw for straight cuts in sheets; a mitre saw for rail ends and the 22.5° and 45° corners; a jigsaw for curves (apple, torch, skaters, skyline); a router for the LED rebate and rounding edges; drill-drivers, a sander, clamps, a level and tape measures. Ask in the quote.

**We provide:**
- A dry, lit workshop with power. About 6 × 8 m works for building one hole at a time.
- A bigger space for the dry run on 18 Dec. The biggest hole is 6 × 3 m, and all ten laid out need a hall.
- A helper each day: someone to hold, carry and sand.
- Extension leads and safety kit: eye and ear protection and dust masks.
- **Volunteers use hand tools and sanders only, unless they're trained on power saws.**

## 6. What we still have to find out (before the carpenter starts)

1. **Door widths and the load-in route:** these set the biggest piece. The pack assumes ≤ 2440 mm long and ≤ 35 kg.
2. **The floor:** what it's made of and how flat it is. Lay a 2 m level and roll a ball across it. Floor-laid holes take the floor's shape.
3. **Ceiling and rig height,** for the 2.4–2.8 m props.
4. **Workshop:** address, access hours, power.
5. **The hole list:** Broadway (drawn) or Rooftop (not drawn) for hole 4.
6. **Full-size artwork** for the cut-outs (apple, torch, skaters, skyline). The art lead draws them, and a print shop prints them full size to trace. The pack's 100 mm grids are enough to quote from.
7. **Who buys the timber,** and how the carpenter is paid (deposit, receipts).
8. **Who signs off:** name the project lead.

## 7. How to use the pack

1. **Send it to 2–3 carpenters this week:** the PDF plus quote template E in `06-rfq-templates.md`. Sheet 2 is the simplified layout, with no power or network details, so it's fine to share. Don't send the cable plan.
2. **Meet the one you like at the venue and the workshop.** Walk the floor, measure the doors together, and show them the 3D model on a laptop.
3. **Ask them to mark up the drawings.** A good carpenter will change things: a different joint, a cheaper sheet size, a stronger base. That's normal, and it's the point of the pack.
4. **Prototype first (by Fri 4 Dec).** Hole 7 or 10, plus one green and one run of rail. Play it hard. Fix the standard details, then build the rest.
5. **After the prototype, every change goes through the project lead.**
6. **At the dry run (18 Dec), check against the rules on sheet 1:** rail height, gaps, nothing tips, edges, labels.

To change the drawings, edit the hole data in `tools/make_carpenter_pack.py` and run `python3 tools/make_carpenter_pack.py --pdf`. The script fails if any gap breaks the 250 mm rule.

## 8. What drawing it out changed in the budget (estimate)

Drawing every part gave real quantities:
- **Rails:** about 140 m (66 lengths of 2.4 m).
- **Raised greens and bevels:** about 28.5 m².
- **Hidden plates:** 182.
- **Plywood:** about 56 sheets (17 × 12 mm, 6 × 18 mm, 9 × 9 mm, 24 × 6 mm).
- **Battens:** about 290 m.

Route C assumed about 24 sheets and 80 m of batten, because it sized only small cup pads. The full-width raised greens, the Grand Central platform, the prop frames and the hidden plates add the rest.

| | Route C plan (before) | From the pack (now) |
|---|---|---|
| Plywood, hardboard and battens | S$1,000 (S$800–1,500) | **≈ S$2,300 (S$1,900–3,000)** |
| Total with 10% contingency | ≈ S$9.5k | **≈ S$10.9k** |

These prices are estimates, not quotes. Singapore plywood has no public list price; the doc 08 assumptions are used.

**Getting back under S$10k:**

| Cut | Saves (S$) |
|---|---|
| Production lends the lights | ≈ 650 |
| Two skyline flats instead of four | ≈ 150 |
| Hidden plates cut from green offcuts | ≈ 100 |
| Borrow the putters | ≈ 150 |

Those four together bring the plan to **≈ S$9.8k**. `11-route-c-carpenter-build.md` is updated to match.

## 9. Glossary

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Arris | A sharp edge. "Ease the arrises" means round them off slightly |
| Batten | A small, square-ish length of timber (here 45 × 45) used for frames |
| Bevel | A slope. Here, the 1:5 ramp up to a raised green |
| Butt joint | Two pieces simply meeting end to face, then screwed |
| Chain dimension | A row of measurements end to end |
| Cleat | A small strip screwed on to stiffen or hold something |
| Countersunk | A screw head sunk below the surface |
| Cut list | The carpenter's list of every piece to cut, with sizes |
| Dressed | Planed smooth on all faces |
| Elevation | A drawing looking straight at the front or side |
| Flat | A painted panel, like theatre scenery |
| Jig | A guide the carpenter makes so volunteers can repeat a cut or fixing exactly |
| Kerf | The width the saw blade removes |
| Mitre | An angled cut, for corners |
| Nominal size | The old name of a timber size ("2 × 4"), bigger than the real one (45 × 95) |
| Offcut | A leftover piece |
| Packer, shim | A thin piece used to lift or level something |
| Plan | A drawing looking down from above |
| Rebate | A step cut along an edge. Here, the groove for the LED strip |
| Section | A drawing as if the part were sawn through |
| Setting out | Marking where things go, from a fixed reference point |
| Skin | A thin sheet glued over a frame |
| Tolerance | How far off a size can be and still work |
