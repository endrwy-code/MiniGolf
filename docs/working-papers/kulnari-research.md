## PART B: Fact-checking the user's beliefs

### B1. "Plastic hardboards with grass glued on: one thick slab of hard plastic with a hole cut in it."
**Verdict: WRONG on the material, PARTLY RIGHT on the hole.**

Photo 1 (grey carpet, two balls):
- The cut edge shows a tan, laminated, chipped and torn edge with several horizontal plies. That is wood sheet (plywood, or ply over particleboard), not plastic. Plastic sheet (PVC foam, HDPE) cuts to a clean, uniform, non-fibrous edge and does not chip in layers. [I, photo]
- Grey needle-punch carpet is glued on top, not turf.
- The hole is not "just a hole in a slab". A white PVC pipe sleeve is set inside the cut and runs down to a rough grey cement or concrete bottom. Either the cup was taken down into the slab or screed, or the void was grouted.
- The cup is deep enough to hold two balls side by side, fully below the surface [I/E].

Photo 2 (turf):
- A purpose-made cup liner (white plastic, putting-green type) with a separate bottom disc. Turf is trimmed to the liner's lip, and the lip sits flush or slightly below the turf.
- Measured off the photo against the 42.7 mm ball, the mouth is about 105–110 mm, i.e. regulation 108 mm [E].
- Perspective shrinks the bottom disc noticeably, so depth is roughly regulation (about 100 mm) or more [E].

Photo 3: the rail is several thin wood strips (about 4 layers, a few mm each) bent to a curve and glued or nailed. It is delaminating and splintered at the free end.

Recommendation: use 18 mm plywood (or 15 mm ply on battens) as the deck. Fit a pipe sleeve or proper cup into an oversize cut, and glue or staple the turf around it. Plastic sheet is dearer than ply in Singapore:
- PVC foam 8 ft: about S$461 per 2-pack [C, search summary, Raptor Supplies]
- HDPE 1 × 0.5 m × 10 mm: S$156 [C, RS]
- Marine ply 8 × 4 ft: S$90 [C hardwarecity.com.sg]

Plastic also flexes, and its only gain is moisture resistance, which a one-night indoor course doesn't need.

### B2. "Our cup doesn't have to be deep; it can slightly stick out; we just need a fast ball that goes in not to roll out."
**Verdict: WRONG on all three counts. Speed is the enemy, a raised rim rejects the best putts, and a shallow hard cup ejects balls.**

Facts:
- **Regulation hole:** 108 mm diameter, at least 101.6 mm deep; any liner sunk at least 25.4 mm below the surface unless soil prevents it [C, USGA definition via search summary]. WMF minigolf rules cap a hole at 120 mm [C dl1/intl2.txt §2.11, WMF 2.3, 2025].
- **Ball:** 42.67 mm minimum diameter; WMF allows balls of 37–43 mm [C].
- **Capture speed** (Holmes 1991, Am. J. Phys. 59:129; Penner 2002, Can. J. Phys. 80:83):
  - The ball must fall by its own radius while crossing the hole: v_max = (2R − r)·√(g/2r), which gives **1.31 m/s** for a dead-centre ball [C via secondary sources; my arithmetic matches].
  - Penner (via Hurrion) gives **1.63 m/s** as the dead-centre limit when the ball may strike the back wall and still drop.
  - Faster balls hit the far lip and bounce out, or run round the rim and jump out [C physics.sydney.edu.au/~cross/GOLF/GOLF.htm].
  - Penner's model assumes putts that would finish about 18 in (0.46 m) past the hole [C search summary].

Capture window versus offset from centre line (simple model, R = 54 mm) [E]:

| Offset b | 0 | 10 mm | 20 mm | 30 mm | 40 mm | 45 mm |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Effective chord | 108 | 106 | 100 | 90 | 73 | 60 mm |
| v_max (m/s) | 1.31 | 1.29 | 1.20 | 1.04 | 0.78 | 0.58 |

With a 110 mm uPVC sleeve (ID about 103.6 mm, see the spec below), every v_max falls about 5–15%: 1.25 m/s at centre, 0.67 at 40 mm.

So a "fast" ball that clips the cup off-centre lips out at about 0.6–0.8 m/s. A ball that would stop 0.45 m past the hole arrives at only **0.64–0.79 m/s** [E, from Stimp 8–12 ft decelerations of 0.45–0.70 m/s²]. Typical mini golf arrival speeds therefore sit right at the capture limit. Slower is better.

**A raised rim:** a rolling ball meeting a step of height h must arrive faster than v_min = √(10gh/7) / (1 − 5h/7r) [E, angular momentum about the edge].

| Step h | 2 | 3 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 mm |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| v_min (m/s) | 0.18 | 0.23 | 0.32 | 0.56 | 0.92 | 1.60 |

Consequences:
1. The gentle "dying" putts, which are the ones most likely to drop, stop against the rim ("leaners") or roll back.
2. Any off-centre contact with a vertical ring deflects the ball sideways.
3. A ball that does climb a 10–15 mm ring hops, and with r = 21 mm it can skip over the far side.

A 10 mm rim roughly halves the useful speed window. If a raised cup is a deliberate gimmick (a "volcano"), ramp up to it with a smooth collar at 10° or less, 40 mm or more long, with no vertical face.

**A shallow, hard bottom** [E]:
- A golf ball on a rigid surface rebounds strongly (restitution coefficient about 0.7–0.8, an estimate).
- If the cup is only about one ball deep (about 45 mm), a ball that drops with forward speed hits the far wall about half a ball below the lip and rides up and out, and a rebound off a hard bottom adds lift.
- The ball must rest fully below the surface (depth > 43 mm). It also needs margin: about 1.5 ball diameters, plus a soft bottom to kill the bounce.

**Recommended cup for a 75 mm deck, with no floor penetration** [E]:
- **Diameter:** 104–108 mm. Use a 110 mm OD uPVC drain pipe off-cut: SS 272 110 mm OD with a 3.2 mm wall gives about 103.6 mm ID [C, pipe table via search summary]. Or use a commercial 108 mm plastic cup cut down.
- **Depth:** 60–68 mm from the turf surface (about 1.4–1.6 ball diameters). Sleeve length = ply thickness + about 45 mm, ending 3–5 mm above the floor.
- **Bottom:** a 6–9 mm ply disc glued and screwed into the sleeve, topped with a 10 mm EVA foam or carpet-offcut pad (no bounce, quiet).
- **Fixing:** a ply hole cut 1 mm oversize. Fix the sleeve with 2–3 glue blocks or cleats under the deck, with its top flush with the ply.
- **Lip:** cut the turf flush to the inside of the sleeve, or slit it and fold the flaps down into the sleeve and glue them. That makes a soft flush lip. Never let the sleeve stand proud. Keep 1–3 mm below flush rather than above.
- **Flat zone:** keep about 300 mm around the cup flat (±1 mm). Put slopes before this zone, not at the cup.
- **Retrieval:** a fingertip gap. A 104 mm hole leaves 61 mm beside a resting ball, which is fine.

### B3. "Their concepts look impressive but are really just wood cut to shape; not super complex."
**Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT about the substrate, WRONG about where the effort and cost go.**

- Kulnari's structures are carpentry: ply decks, a laminated-strip rail, wooden crates and props (photos) [I].
- But the result comes from layers non-carpenters underestimate:
  1. **Concept, story and creative direction:** an outside creative-direction contract ran about 15 months (UNVERIFIED) [LinkedIn].
  2. **Scenic finishing:** aging, dark-stained "teak-style" paint, riveted faux-steel walls [C GirlStyle; photo 3].
  3. **Set dressing:** "heaps of vintage artefacts and antiques" [C GirlStyle]. Punchdrunk-style sourcing works the same way: props from "antique fairs… junk stores" [C summary].
  4. **Lighting, sound, fog and strobe** [C K-home].
  5. **Mechanisms:** a spinning platform, a boat, a slide.
  6. **Durability treatment:** scenic studios coat EPS foam carvings in polyurethane or polyurea hard-coats (about 65–75 Shore D) before painting, because bare foam crumbles. One UK team uses "strong renders and varnishes" against stray balls. [C, search summaries]
- Industry per-hole budgets for themed indoor holes are US$15–35k (SEO-grade sources; treat as an order of magnitude) [C, search summary]. The S$10k for 10 holes here is about 2–3% of that.
- Typical split for a one-night build [E]: about 30–40% of hours on carpentry. The rest goes to design and mock-ups, sanding, priming and painting (2 coats, plus drying time in Singapore humidity), signs and graphics, lighting, play-testing and fixes.
- Recommendation: cut-to-shape ply is the right medium. Budget at least as many volunteer-hours for paint, graphics and lighting as for cutting. One big, bold painted silhouette per hole beats many small props. In dim light, contrast and lighting do the work.

### B4. Build methods that survive one night with crowds [mostly E, with constraints C where noted]

**Curved guide rails**
- Minimum bend radius for dry plywood bent across the grain [C, APA table via Fine Homebuilding]: 6 mm (1/4") about 0.6 m; 9 mm (3/8") about 0.9 m. Parallel to the grain: 1.5 m and 2.4 m.
- For tighter curves, use one of:
  - (a) Laminate 3–4 strips of 3–4 mm ply or MDF with PVA and brad nails over a cut-ply former. This is Kulnari's method; photo 3 shows the free end peeling.
  - (b) Kerf-cut 18 mm ply, leaving 3 mm. Kerf spacing comes from a calculator plus a test piece [C].
  - (c) Stack 2–3 layers of 18 mm ply arcs cut with a jigsaw: rigid, no bending, strong.
- Height: no commercial standard was found [C, search, nothing]. Use 70–90 mm above the playing surface (about 2 ball diameters) to stop hop-outs.
- Fix rails every 300 mm into the deck. Cap and screw every free end; that is where Kulnari's failed. Face rails with 3–5 mm EVA or rubber for consistent rebound. Round the top edges for shins.

**Plinko / pegboard for golf balls**
- Rule of thumb: the ball should be about 60% of the horizontal peg pitch, so about 71 mm centre-to-centre for a 42.7 mm ball [C engineerfix.com rule; my arithmetic].
- With 10–12 mm dowels or bolts that leaves a clear gap of about 59–61 mm (1.4× the ball): no jams, real randomness. Vertical row pitch about 60–65 mm, rows offset by half a pitch.
- Pegs must stand at least 35 mm proud, above the ball's 21 mm centre height, so balls can't ride over. Embed them 15–20 mm in drilled ply, glue, and add a rear batten.
- Board angle: a rolling ball needs (5/7)·g·sinθ greater than surface drag. That is about 4–6° on turf (from Stimp deceleration) and less on bare painted ply [E].
  - Use 10–15° for a lively bare or painted board, laid open on the deck. The ball enters at the top from a putted ramp or tee.
  - Steeper than about 20° needs a clear acrylic cover (Puttshack's is an "uphill putt into a covered plinko board" [C summary]).
- Exit slots at least 55 mm wide, each feeding a scoring lane or the cup.
- Seal the paint so pegs can't splinter.

**Multi-tube trick tunnels**
- WMF's pipe lane uses a 45–65 mm pipe [C banemaal.txt]. No jam data was found [C, search].
- Use 75–90 mm ID (3" pipe) or the 110 mm drain pipe (ID about 104 mm) for anything with bends. Use sweep or 45° bends, never short 90° elbows. 50 mm pipe (ID about 47–52 mm) only suits short straight runs [E].
- Fall: 2–5° in smooth PVC. Mouth flared or chamfered and set flush with the deck (any lip over 2–3 mm stops slow balls; see the step table in B2).
- Crossovers: one tube passes over another with at least 10 mm clearance, mounted in notched ply saddles.
- Retrievability:
  - Every tube must be pushable end to end with a putter or a broom handle, so straight runs of 1.5 m or less.
  - Removable slip-on cap or clear section at bends.
  - Cut a 15 mm sight slot, or use clear acrylic tube for one route: players love seeing the ball.
- Random exits (the Harris "pipe shot": "you think it's coming out in one spot and comes out in another" [C summary]) are cheap drama.
- Cable-tie or screw tubes to blocking. Crowds lean on them.

**Curling-style target**
- A flat, level (±2 mm), smooth landing area with painted or printed rings. Example: 1.5 m house with rings at 0.3 / 0.75 / 1.5 m scoring 3/2/1, plus a centre cup for an ace bonus [E].
- Add a gentle uphill run-out (about 3–5°) or a soft backstop so overpowered balls stop or return rather than leave.
- Other balls are allowed to stay and be knocked (curling spectacle). Use ring-points scoring (bullseye formats [C summary]).
- Pace risk: everyone waits for balls to stop, so allow 1 shot per player.

**Two-sided rally hill with goal on top** (WMF lane 16, "volcano" [C banemaal]; Mt Holey Moley [C summary])
- Minimum speed to crest height H: v ≥ √(10gH/7) plus drag. H = 150 mm needs about 1.45 m/s; 200 mm needs about 1.67 m/s [E].
- Keep H at 120–180 mm and slopes at 10–15°. Radius the foot and crest transitions (at least 300 mm, or a bent-ply skin over ribs) so balls don't launch.
- Flat plateau 350–450 mm across with the cup centred and a 10–15 mm raised soft ring 40 mm outside it. Opposing groups putt from both ends.
- A failed putt rolls back to the player: self-returning, good for pace.
- Steeper than about 20° turns into a power lottery.

**Ramps between decks of different height**
- Ball side: 12° or less, joints sanded flush. Any step over 2–3 mm facing the ball stops or deflects slow putts (0.18–0.23 m/s threshold). Downhill steps make the ball hop.
- People side: the 75 mm deck edge is a trip line. Chamfer every exposed edge, add contrasting or glow tape, and give walk-on routes a 1:12 ramp. The ADA mini golf guide only allows a 1-inch curb on accessible routes [C access-board.gov chapter 10].

**Turf versus carpet**
- No lab comparison exists [C, search]. Vendors say short, dense, no-infill golf turf (6–10 mm) rolls true; long landscape turf is slow and inconsistent [C summary].
- Needle-punch exhibition carpet (Kulnari photo 1) is fast and smooth but shows seams and ripples.
- Local prices: EcoPutt 8 mm about S$13.20/m² [C Carousell via summary].
- Use one surface everywhere so players calibrate once. Lay it taut, wrapped and stapled round the deck edges [C DIY guides].
- Test speed with a DIY Stimpmeter: a notched ramp at 20°, release point 76 cm, about 1.83 m/s release [C summary]. Aim for 2.4–3.0 m (8–10 ft): slow enough for capture and short run-outs.

### B5. What non-experts usually get wrong [E, with Kulnari evidence noted]
1. **Pace:** they design holes for fun, not for 2–2.5 minutes. One hard hole backs up every group behind it [C summary]. This is Kulnari's top complaint (Part A).
2. **Seams and level:** uneven decks or joints cause drift (Kulnari "uneven flooring… ball drift"). Shim decks on the auditorium floor and check with a 2 m straightedge and a ball.
3. **Cup:** too shallow, hard-bottomed or proud (see B2).
4. **Rails:** too low, ends unfixed, gaps at joints. Balls escape into the crowd and get lost.
5. **Tubes and mechanisms that jam or break** under hundreds of uses, with no reset access. A staff-crank mechanism (Pixar Putt Up [C summary]) is reliable; player-operated levers are not.
6. **Unreadable holes and blind cups without a hint:** "challenging… but it shouldn't be confusing" [C Loftus]. "An ace must be feasible" [C GolfPass].
7. **Lighting too dim for reading and footing.** Kulnari's "small print in the dark".
8. **Underestimating finishing time** (paint drying, graphics) and play-testing.
9. **Trip edges, props in walkways, sharp corners at shin height.**
10. **Fire and permit rules:** drapes, fog and decorations. SCDF needs a Temporary Change of Use Permit for events, applied for at least 3 working days ahead, under Indoor Event Fire Safety Conditions (updated 19 Feb 2026) [C summary]. Check with the venue whether this auditorium event triggers it. Use flame-retardant fabric (BS 5867-type standards are referenced [C]). Skip fog: smoke-detector risk.
11. **Ball loss and turnover:** no colour-coded balls, no spare stock, nobody posted to reset holes.

## PART C: Design knowledge

### C1. Principles of good hole design (with examples)
- **Ace feasible, risk graded:** "an ace must be feasible"; good putts score, bad ones carry risk [C GolfPass/Gavrich].
- **Risk and reward:** a pin in the middle of a creek "because of the risk reward" [C UDisc/Morgan Wright]. "Two-triangle design where you go for it, or you don't" [C Pizá].
- **Route choice:** a safe long path versus a risky short gap or tube. Puttshack "Supertubes" are high-jackpot shortcuts with hazard risk [C summary].
- **Readable over blind:** Swingers' designer wants players to "work out how the ball will behave and read the angles easily… skills tested, but not too much" [C summary, Tom Borer].
  - Blind or random holes work as occasional seasoning: Walker holes "rely heavily on elements of chance", e.g. pachinko and multi-exit tunnels [C].
  - Chance helps weaker putters; at a party it equalises kids and adults [I].
- **Frustration control:** "A hole can be challenging and involve a lot of steps but it shouldn't be confusing"; "fashion over function shouldn't result in the lack of a hole" [C Loftus, A Couple of Putts]. "Not so hard that kids get frustrated" [C Harris/Onyshczak]. Pixar Putt was criticised for favouring IP over playability, with balls careening "down the stairs" [C summary].
- **Difficulty curve:** a gradual increase [C guide]. Kulnari's harder back nine was praised [C Lemon8]. For 10 holes: holes 1–2 easy (teach), 3–7 varied, 8–9 a spike, 10 a friendly spectacle finale [E].
- **Signature or finale holes:**
  - Pixar Putt hole 18: a staff-cranked flying Up house [C].
  - Shipwrecked Brooklyn: the final hole is a subway car where you deposit the ball in a MetroCard machine [C].
  - Kulnari: a surprise slide and the reveal [C].
- **Animatronics and props** should "add decoration rather than interfere with the play" [C Schwartzman].
- **Social spectacle:**
  - Players as obstacles ("Be Your Own Sculpture"); "Move Your Hole!" on opponents; a shared green where groups putt simultaneously ("18 Holes in One") [C Walker summary].
  - Sound feedback, e.g. chimes or xylophone holes at Figment [C].
  - Points and bonuses (Puttshack) keep everyone in it [C].

### C2. Pace of play and throughput

Commercial benchmarks:

| Venue | Format | Time | Per hole | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Puttshack | 9 holes | about 30 min | about 3.3 min | [C FAQ] |
| Monster Mini Golf | 18 holes | 45–60 min | 2.5–3.3 min | [C] |
| Swingers | course designed to finish in 40 min ("before getting bored"), simple holes | 40 min | — | [C] |
| UltraGolf / Dino Putt (SG) | 18 holes | about 1 h | about 3.3 min | [C Honeycombers] |
| General sources | 18 holes, 3–4 players | 1–1.5 h | 3.3–5 min | [C summaries] |
| Kulnari (official) | 18 holes | 1–2 h | 3.3–6.7 min | [C] |
| User's visit, Kulnari | 18 holes, 4 players | 2 h | 6.7 min | — |

- How operators keep pace [C]:
  - stroke caps (5–7; Kulnari 6)
  - capped entry (Kulnari 10 per slot, staggered 5-minute starts, a daily cap)
  - simple holes (Swingers' brief)
  - automatic scoring (Puttshack)
  - marshals (Kulnari staff hand out puzzle answers to move people on)
  - a mid-round bar to absorb queues
- **What Kulnari's 6.7 min/hole means:** it is a slow, story-and-reading pace by design, and it still congests.
  - Kulnari's entry rate is about 40 players/h. Your target of 230–270 players in 3 h is **77–90 players/h: about 2× Kulnari's rate with about half the holes** [E].
- **Party maths:** every group plays every hole and each hole holds one group, so throughput = 60 / (minutes per group at the slowest hole) [E].
  - At 4 per group, 77–90 players/h = 19–23 groups/h, so the slowest hole must clear a group in **≤ 2.6–3.1 min**.
  - Two loop districts from the hub help routing and congestion, but don't raise per-hole capacity if everyone plays all 10.
  - 4 players × about 2.5 strokes = about 10 strokes. At about 10 s each plus walking and retrieval, that's about 2–2.5 min: feasible only with:
    - a 4–5 stroke cap ("pick up after 5")
    - holes 3–6 m long with ace-feasible lines
    - self-returning or short-reset hazards
    - no reading; signs readable in 3 s
    - 1 shot each on target or curling holes
    - tee-offs released every 2.5–3 min from the hub by a marshal
    - a marshal floating at the 2 hardest holes
  - Fallbacks if queues build:
    - groups of 5
    - "play one district (5 holes)" tickets at peak
    - a duplicate of the slowest hole
    - a bypass rule (skip a hole if 2+ groups are waiting)
  - Round time is about 25–30 min including walking [E].

### C3. "Small rooms make vibe easier (The Last of Us, linear) vs open world (GTA 6)"
**Evaluation: PARTLY RIGHT.**
- Enclosure does make immersion easier:
  - Disney's berm blocks outside views and sound to create "a more intimate setting" [C summary].
  - Hench: "eliminate any visual contradictions" [C summary].
  - Punchdrunk pairs small disorienting rooms with "long shot" spaces [C summary].
  - Kulnari works because each room hides the others [I].
- But the analogy overstates the trade-off:
  - Naughty Dog calls The Last of Us Part II "wide linear": a guided spine with large open pockets [C summary]. That is exactly a hub-and-loop course.
  - Open worlds use hide-and-reveal too: BOTW's "triangle rule" hides content behind landforms and reveals it on approach [C summary].
- **Big-room advantages:**
  - One shared visual magnet, Disney's "weenie" [C]: the stage and countdown, a Times Square tower.
  - Collective energy and noise at midnight; spectators watching signature holes.
  - Staff sightlines and safety; simple egress.
  - Flexible routing; one lighting rig.
- **Techniques for intimate zones in one night** [E, building on C summaries]:
  1. Dim the house lights and light each hole in its own pool ("Light creates a space… to create an intimate space, take down the lighting" [C]). Let darkness be the wall.
  2. Back every hole with a 1.8–2.4 m painted flat or backdrop facing the player (a storefront, subway tiles), so players see their hole, not the others. Hinged flats or pipe-and-drape need no rigging. Use flame-retardant fabric.
  3. Lower the ceiling locally with string-light canopies or overhead "billboards" over each district [C, the event-design advice].
  4. Change the floor per district (carpet colour or graphics), with threshold portals: a subway-entrance arch, a theatre marquee.
  5. Small local sound per zone at low volume: traffic, subway, Broadway, Central Park birds.
  6. Hide and reveal: route paths so each signature prop is seen on the turn, with the skyline weenie always visible above the flats.
  7. Keep sightlines from the hub to every queue so marshals can see backups.

### C4. NYC motifs: safe versus avoid [C facts; risk judgement is E. Private, non-commercial church event, low risk, but photos will go online.]
**Safe or low risk:**
- **Statue of Liberty:** copyright expired, public domain [C, Library of Congress / NPS].
  - Base imagery on the original statue or your own drawing. Do not copy photos of the Las Vegas replica: a court awarded US$3.55M against USPS for using the replica's face [C].
- **Generic city elements:** a generic skyline or Art Deco spires (not an exact ESB rendering), yellow cabs without TLC marks, generic subway cars and tiles, hot dog and pretzel carts, fire hydrants, water towers, fire escapes, brownstones, pizza slices, bagels, steaming manholes, green street signs, Broadway-style marquees with invented show names, Central Park benches, the Brooklyn Bridge silhouette, and a generic "countdown ball".
**Avoid or alter:**
- "I ♥ NY": the state's trademark, actively enforced (Everyman Espresso, 2013; licensing revenue) [C].
- MTA logo and subway bullets: MTA licenses and sends cease-and-desists. Protectability of the bullets is disputed. Use invented line symbols (e.g. a "2027" line in your own shape) [C].
- The Empire State Building name or exact silhouette as a branded element: the owner holds image registrations and licenses them; TTAB found its image famous [C].
- "NYC Taxi" / TLC marks and the "T" logo: the City claims NYC as a trademark [C].
- NYPD and FDNY shields and names: city-licensed and litigated [C].
- "Times Square New Year's Eve Ball" wording: trademark filings by Jamestown One Times Square and the Times Square District Management Association. Say "countdown ball" instead [C].
- Real advertiser logos on "Times Square billboards": use parody or invented church-friendly brands.
